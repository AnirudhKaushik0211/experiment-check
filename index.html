Guide

What this does

ExperimentCheck helps you plan an A/B test before it starts: how many users you need, how long to run it and what the test can detect. After the test, it audits the observed numbers with standard checks such as the sample ratio, timing and guardrails. It then gives a documented verdict and writes a pre-registration and a decision memo you can share.

Quick start

  1. Plan: enter the baseline, the minimum detectable effect, alpha, power, the split and your daily traffic.
  2. Pre-register: copy or download the pre-registration and agree on it before the test starts.
  3. Share: copy the share link at the bottom of the page. It holds all your inputs.
  4. Read out: after the planned end date, enter the observed users, conversions or means, and the guardrail numbers.
  5. Decide: read the verdict and the checks, then copy, download or print the decision memo.

Try an example

  • Significant lift above the MDE, clean checks.
  • Ran to plan, not significant, and the interval still includes the MDE.
  • Treatment has 900 fewer users than control under a 50/50 split.
  • Analysed on day 5 of 14 after three looks.
  • Huge sample, significant, but the whole interval is below the MDE.
  • Conversion is up, but refunds rose by more than the limit.

How to read the verdict

The verdict follows a fixed rule, checked in this order. The first rule that applies decides.

  1. Investigate (SRM): the traffic split does not match the plan. Every metric is suspect until you find the cause.
  2. Don't ship: a guardrail got worse by more than its pre-registered limit, however good the primary metric looks.
  3. Inconclusive - extend: the data end before the planned end date. Early looks inflate false positives, so the result is not final.
  4. Otherwise the primary metric decides. Ship when it improved significantly and by at least the MDE. Don't ship when it got significantly worse, when the whole interval is below the MDE, or when the interval rules out an improvement as large as the MDE. In every other case the result is inconclusive: extend the test or re-plan.

Glossary

A/B test
A randomised experiment that shows one version (control) to some users and a changed version (treatment) to others, then compares a metric between the two groups.
Control and treatment
The two groups, or arms, of the test. Control sees the current experience; treatment sees the change.
Baseline
The value of the metric today, without the change. The plan uses it to work out the sample size.
Conversion rate
The share of users who did the thing you measure, such as buying or signing up. 1,540 conversions out of 15,400 users is a 10.00% rate.
Minimum detectable effect, MDE
The smallest change worth detecting. The test is sized so that a true effect of this size is found with the chosen power. It is also the bar an effect must clear to count as meaningful.
Absolute vs relative effect, pp
An absolute effect is the plain difference, in percentage points (pp) for rates. A relative effect is that difference divided by the baseline. From 10% to 11% is +1 pp absolute and +10% relative.
Significance level, alpha
The false-positive rate you accept: the chance of calling an effect significant when there is none. 5% is common.
Power
The chance that the test finds a true effect as large as the MDE. 80% is common. A test with fewer users than planned has less power.
One- vs two-sided test
A two-sided test looks for a change in either direction. A one-sided test only looks for an improvement, so it needs fewer users but can't show harm.
p-value
How surprising the observed difference would be if the change had no effect. A p-value below alpha is called significant. It is not the chance that the change works.
Confidence interval
A range of effects that fit the data. A 95% interval built this way contains the true effect in about 95% of tests. If the whole interval is below zero, the change is harmful.
Practical significance
Whether an effect is large enough to matter. Here that means at least the MDE. An effect can be statistically significant and still too small to be worth shipping.
Sample ratio mismatch, SRM
The arms received a different share of users than planned. It usually means a bug in assignment or logging, and it makes every metric untrustworthy.
Peeking
Looking at results before the planned end and being ready to stop on a good number. Each extra look raises the real false-positive rate above alpha.
Alpha spending / O'Brien-Fleming
A way to plan interim looks in advance by spending a small part of alpha early and most of it at the end. The figure shown here is informational only.
Guardrail metric
A metric that must not get worse, such as refunds or page load time. A guardrail breach blocks shipping even when the primary metric improves.
Maximum acceptable degradation
How much worse a guardrail may get, in percent of its control value, before the change is blocked.
Novelty and day-of-week effects
Users react to anything new at first, and behaviour differs by weekday. Running whole weeks, and at least one week, reduces both.
Pre-registration
Writing down the hypothesis, metric, sample, duration and decision rule before the test starts, so the analysis can't be bent to fit the result.
Two-proportion z-test
The standard test for comparing two conversion rates. It uses a normal approximation, which is unreliable with fewer than about 10 events or non-events in an arm.
Welch's t-test
A test for comparing two means that does not assume equal variances. It relies on the sample means being roughly normal, which needs enough users per arm.
Chi-square goodness-of-fit
A test of whether observed counts match expected shares. Here it checks the traffic split for a sample ratio mismatch.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Users are randomised independently, the analysis unit equals the randomisation unit, and each user is counted once.
  • Normal approximations: z-test, Wald CI and Katz log CI (binary); delta-method relative CI (continuous); normal-approximation sample size. They are unreliable with small counts, and the tool warns.
  • Welch's t-test assumes approximately normal sample means (central limit theorem). Heavily skewed metrics (revenue) may need more data or a transformation.
  • One treatment vs one control. No correction for multiple metrics or multiple guardrails.
  • No valid sequential testing: peeking is only warned about. The O'Brien-Fleming figure is informational.
  • The verdict is a documented rule, not a substitute for judgement about costs, risks and strategy.
  • Daily traffic is assumed constant. Returning users make the real duration longer.

Privacy

  • No network requests (blocked by a Content Security Policy and checked by automated tests).
  • Nothing is uploaded. All numbers are computed in your browser.
  • The share link contains your inputs. Browsers never send the part after # to a server, but anyone with the link can read the inputs, so share it only where they may be seen.
  • localStorage holds only one flag: whether you have seen the Guide.
